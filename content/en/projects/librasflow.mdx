## Context and problem

The Libras manual alphabet is used to spell names and words that have no sign of their own. LibrasFlow, my capstone project, recognizes those letters through a webcam, in real time, on an ordinary computer, and spells words on screen.

The first model looked done: on the test set, it got practically every letter right. That too-perfect result was the problem. A classifier that never fails at a hard visual task is usually being tested on data it has already seen.

## My role

I built the whole pipeline: extracting hand landmarks from the images, training the classifier, auditing and fixing the evaluation, validating on real images and the app that spells words through the camera.

## How it works

The model doesn't look at the raw image. MediaPipe finds 21 hand landmarks, and the classifier works only with their geometry. That keeps the system light and less sensitive to background and lighting.

Feature extraction lives in a single module used by data collection, training, validation and the app. That way the normalization never differs between what the model saw in training and what it gets live. The interface runs in the browser (Flask streaming the camera feed) and has a virtual "Clear" button, triggered by holding a hand over it.

## The data leak

The training images came from bursts: dozens of near-identical frames of the same gesture. With a random split, one frame went to training and its almost identical neighbor went to test. The model wasn't generalizing; it was recognizing again.

To confirm it, I measured the distance from each test sample to the closest one in training, within the same letter. Many had a near-clone on the other side.

The script checks that no group appears on both sides before training. The first version of the split picked 20% of each letter's groups, which broke when groups had very different sizes: with a larger radius (0.10), a single group of the letter G held 900 of its 1,650 samples. The current version picks test groups to get close to 20% of the samples, not of the groups.

Even so, accuracy on the new test stayed very high. With no leak between training and test, both parts still come from the same set of images. To know how the model behaves outside it, I needed a test on images it had never seen.

## Validation on real images

I ran the final model on a separate folder of real images. The hand was detected in 3,812 of them, covering 15 of the 21 letters, and overall accuracy was **69%**.

- **Almost always right:** B, V, A, I, W and D, with recall above 95%. C and O are close to 90%.
- **Almost always wrong:** E, M and N were never recognized; S and U got about 5% right, and R, 10%. L reached 44%.
- **Where the errors go:** many land on V, which has 100% recall but 40% precision.

The letters that fail most depend on bent or crossed fingers, where the difference is which finger is in front. That is why the z coordinate went into the features, and it is where the capstone continues. A diagnostic script saves the misclassified images with the landmarks drawn, to tell whether the problem is hand detection or classification.

## Stack

- **Computer vision:** MediaPipe Hands and OpenCV.
- **Model and data:** scikit-learn (Random Forest and DBSCAN), pandas and NumPy.
- **App:** Flask, with the webcam feed processed on the server.

## Decisions and trade-offs

<Decision
  title="Hand landmarks instead of the image"
  tradeoff="If MediaPipe doesn't detect the hand, there is no prediction. Some real-world errors may come from there, not from the classifier."
>
  With 63 numbers per frame, the model is small, runs in real time on an ordinary computer and doesn't depend on the image background.
</Decision>

<Decision
  title="Group before splitting"
  tradeoff="The result depends on the grouping radius. At 0.05, every letter has enough groups; larger radii leave some letters with few groups and an unstable test."
>
  Splitting by row measures memory. Splitting by pose measures whether the model recognizes a gesture it has never seen.
</Decision>

<Decision
  title="Measure and train in separate scripts"
  tradeoff="The app's model has no test number of its own; its reference is the group split and the real-image validation."
>
  One script holds data out to measure accuracy. After that, the model that goes into the app trains on 100% of the data.
</Decision>

<Decision
  title="Confirm a letter only after 30 frames"
  tradeoff="Spelling is slower: each letter requires holding the sign for a moment."
>
  It keeps the phrase from filling up with wrong letters while the hand moves from one sign to the next.
</Decision>

## Outcome

- The app works in real time and spells words through the webcam.
- The evaluation no longer relies on repeated frames: it now splits data by pose and goes through real images.
- It is clear where the model fails (E, M, N, R, S and U) and why. That guides the next stage of the capstone.

Next steps: more varied images for the letters that fail, the letters that involve movement (such as J, K, X and Z) and recognition with both hands.

## What I learned

- Perfect accuracy is a warning sign, not a success.
- How the data is split matters as much as the model.
- The test that counts is the one with data that looks like real use.
